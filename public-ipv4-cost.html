🌐 Public IPv4 Cost

An address is now a metered resource in its own right: $0.005 per hour, which is $3.65 a month and $43.80 a year. The rate does not care whether the address is serving traffic or sitting in a subnet doing nothing, and it does not vary by region or by instance size. That single change inverted a decade of advice — attaching an Elastic IP used to stop the meter, and now it only moves it.

Contiguous blocks are a third line item at a higher rate.

Standard addresses are priced at $0.005/hour and contiguous-block addresses at $0.008/hour; the two are totalled separately and added, because a block is billed at its own rate regardless of whether its addresses are assigned.

What the meter actually counts

AWS prices a public IPv4 address the way a landlord prices a parking space: per space per hour, whether or not a car is in it. The published rate is $0.005 per address per hour for in-use addresses and $0.005 per address per hour for idle ones. There is no volume discount, no region variation, and no difference between an address on a production load balancer and one attached to a stopped instance.

monthly = addresses × $0.005/hr × 730 hr = addresses × $3.65 annual = addresses × $0.005/hr × 8,760 hr = addresses × $43.80

The 730-hour month is the billing convention, not a calendar fact: (365 × 24) ÷ 12 = 730. A 30-day month bills 720 hours and a 31-day month bills 744, which is why a bill that says $3.65 in February can say $3.72 in March. Over a year the two bases converge exactly, because 730 × 12 = 8,760.

The bill scales with topology, not with traffic. Ten addresses cost $36.50 a month whether the fleet serves a thousand requests or a billion, because the meter counts sockets in the network design rather than bytes on the wire.

The advice that stopped working in February 2024

Until 2024 the charge applied only to addresses that were not attached to a running resource. That produced a piece of folk knowledge repeated in every cost-optimization checklist: if you are paying for an Elastic IP, attach it to something and the charge goes away. The instruction was correct, and it trained a generation of engineers to think of attachment as the fix.

Since February 2024 both states carry the same rate. The old remedy now converts one $3.65 charge into a different $3.65 charge:

The only row that changed the answer is the last one. Releasing is now the entire optimization, and it is the one step the old checklist never asked for, because the old meter had already taught everyone that the address was free once it was in use.

This is the same shape as the index advice elsewhere on this site: a rule that was true when it was written, still repeated after the constraint underneath it moved. The check is not whether the advice sounds right, but whether the number it was derived from is still the number.

What a 60-second minimum does to churn

The bill is calculated in one-second increments with a minimum of 60 seconds. For a steady fleet that floor never binds: an address held for a month is billed for a month. It binds on the workloads that allocate and release addresses constantly — integration-test environments, blue-green cutovers, per-tenant gateways spun up on demand.

A single one-minute allocation costs $0.005 ÷ 60 = $0.0000833, which is to say nothing. Ten thousand of them cost $0.83. The reason to know the rule is not the arithmetic; it is that a cost model built on "addresses × hours" silently drops this term, and then someone discovers a line item that the model said could not exist. Round up every allocation event to a minute, then add it to the hourly total.

Contiguous blocks cost 1.6× and buy something else

An Amazon-provided contiguous IPv4 block is priced at $0.008 per address per hour, not $0.005. You are not buying more addresses; you are buying the property that they are sequential, which is what makes firewall allowlisting and BGP announcements possible without maintaining a list of 256 unrelated entries.

Two details matter more than the 1.6×. First, every address in the block is charged from the moment the block is provisioned, and the in-use / idle distinction does not apply inside it — there is nothing to be gained by assigning them. Second, the block is billed in hourly increments with any partial hour counted as a full hour, so the 60-second floor that applies to single addresses does not apply here; the granularity is coarser.

What is not on the meter

Three categories are excluded, and each is a legitimate way to move addresses off the bill rather than merely reorganising them.

Bring Your Own IP. Address space you have registered with a regional internet registry and imported into AWS is not charged the public IPv4 rate. The trade is that you now own the RIR relationship, the annual fees and the minimum assignment size that comes with it — a /24 in most regions, which is 256 addresses to justify before the first one is used.

Customer-owned IP on Outposts. Addresses in a customer-owned pool are not billed at the public IPv4 rate either, for the same reason: AWS is not supplying them.

Addresses that are not dedicated to your resource. When a service shares its front end across customers, as S3 does, the address is not yours and is not metered to you. A static site on S3 plus CloudFront can therefore serve public traffic while holding zero public IPv4 addresses of its own — which is the pattern worth checking before assuming an internet-facing workload must cost $3.65 a month per endpoint.

The last one generalises: the cheapest address is the one the architecture never needed. A load balancer that terminates TLS for six services holds one address; six load balancers hold six. Consolidating front ends is an architecture decision that happens to have a price, and at $3.65 a month per endpoint it is small enough to be ignored in review and large enough to appear on the bill every month for years.

Modelling assumptions

  • Month length. 730 hours is used for monthly figures, the standard billing convention of (365 × 24) ÷ 12. Real months bill 672 to 744 hours, so a single month will differ from the projection by up to 2%.
  • Year length. 8,760 hours, which is exactly 730 × 12, so the monthly and annual columns never disagree.
  • Steady state. Addresses are assumed held for the whole period. Allocation churn is not modelled beyond the note on the 60-second minimum.
  • Excluded. Data transfer, NAT gateway hourly and per-GB charges, load balancer hours, and any charge that the address merely accompanies. This page prices addresses only.
  • Excluded. Enterprise discounts, private pricing agreements, credits and refunds. Public IPv4 pricing is not commonly discountable, but a negotiated bill may differ.
